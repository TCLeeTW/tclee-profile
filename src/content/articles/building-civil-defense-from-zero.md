In July 2025, a small group of us in Taipei's Neihu and Ganghu districts decided to stop waiting for someone else to organize civil preparedness in our neighborhood. We started 港湖民防團 — a volunteer civil defense team built from scratch, with no institutional backing and no model to copy.

Eight months later, we have weekly training sessions, a structured curriculum across medical, communications, and physical fitness tracks, and a community that's actively grappling with what it means to be prepared. We've also learned a lot about what makes this hard.

## Why community-level, and why now

Taiwan's civil defense discussion tends to happen at the national policy level or in abstract terms about "whole-of-society defense." What's missing is the neighborhood layer — the people who would actually help each other in the first 72 hours after a major event, before institutions can respond at scale.

This isn't unique to Taiwan. Ukraine's experience showed that communities with pre-existing mutual aid structures — informal networks, people who knew each other and had practiced together — fared meaningfully better than those without. The formal military and civil defense apparatus matters, but the foundation is human networks.

The timing in Taiwan is obvious to anyone paying attention. The risk environment has shifted. More people feel it, even if they don't act on it. We started because we thought: if not now, when?

## What we actually do

The team runs weekly meetings and quarterly training events. We've built three functional tracks:

**Medical** — basic first aid, tourniquet application, trauma assessment. We're working toward team-level TCCC competency. Several members have completed EMT-1 certification.

**Communications** — amateur radio licensing, repeater network mapping, mesh networking concepts. If cell infrastructure goes down, this is how teams coordinate.

**Physical** — not military fitness, but the practical kind: carrying weight, navigating unfamiliar terrain, working effectively when tired. This gets underestimated.

We've also started running scenario exercises — tabletop at first, then field exercises. The scenarios are deliberately local and specific. Not "a war breaks out" but "a large earthquake damages the bridge on [street], the hospital ER is overwhelmed, you have three hours before outside help arrives."

## What's hard

**Sustaining attention.** Most people who show up to the first meeting don't come back. This isn't cynicism — it's the reality of any volunteer organization. The hard work is designing a structure that rewards continued participation and creates genuine social bonds, not just a training class people attend once.

**Calibrating the message.** Civil defense in Taiwan is politically and emotionally loaded. Some people hear "civil defense" and think military conscription. Others hear it and think political statement. We've had to develop language that focuses on community resilience and mutual aid — which is accurate and also more universally accessible.

**Knowledge integration.** We have members who are medical professionals, engineers, teachers, and retirees. Getting domain knowledge from experts into a format that non-experts can actually use under stress is a real curriculum design problem. Most civil defense materials were written either for professionals or for a very general public audience. The middle layer — competent, trained volunteers — has thin resource coverage.

## What I'm researching

One of the questions I keep coming back to: *what actually causes people to prepare?* Not in a theoretical public health sense, but specifically — what changes between someone thinking "I should be more prepared" and actually doing something about it?

I'm doing structured research on this question. The early hypothesis is that perceived community efficacy matters more than individual risk perception. People don't prepare because they feel personally at risk; they prepare because they believe their community can actually do something about it, and they're part of that community.

That has implications for how civil defense organizations should recruit, message, and structure their programs. We're testing some of those implications in real time with 港湖民防團.

More on this as the research develops.
